01 · The puzzleSame tombs, far apart

Walk into a passage grave in Brittany, then one in the Boyne valley in Ireland, then one on Orkney, in northern Portugal or on a Danish island. The details differ, but the idea is the same: a long, low passage of upright slabs, roofed with stones that took dozens of people to move, leading into a chamber for the dead. Dolmens, gallery graves and standing stones repeat the trick from Andalusia to Sweden.

Why would farmers a thousand miles apart build the same thing? One old answer is that the idea travelled by boat, along the Atlantic coast. In 2019 Bettina Schulz Paulsson modelled more than 2,400 radiocarbon dates and argued in PNAS that the first megalithic graves appeared in north-west France and that the tradition spread from there, in a few phases, by sea.

I wanted to see what open data can say about that story. So I gathered every megalithic monument that volunteers have mapped in OpenStreetMap, {{data.megaliths|int}} of them after cleaning, and set them three tests. Do megaliths sit closer to the sea than chance, and closer than other prehistoric burial mounds? Do the oldest radiocarbon dates from megalithic sites sit on the Atlantic coast? And is the Atlantic facade really where the great stone tombs are, type by type?

The short answer: the sea-route story survives all three tests, but none of them proves it, and one of them is much weaker than it first looks.

    03 · Test oneDo megaliths hug the sea?

    If the idea came by boat, you might expect the monuments to crowd the coast. They do, compared with random ground. Of all {{data.megaliths|int}} monuments, {{coast.groups.all.observed_near|pct0}} stand within 10 km of the sea. Ten random points on land for every monument, in the same countries, put only {{coast.groups.all.random_near|pct0}} that close. That makes megaliths {{coast.groups.all.ratio_random|f1}} times as likely to be near the sea as the land they sit in, and the pattern holds in {{coast.countries_closer_than_random}} of the {{coast.countries_tested}} countries with at least 50 monuments.

    But random land is a soft target. People in prehistory did not live at random, and coasts and river mouths are good places to farm, fish and travel whatever you build for your dead. So I used a second, tougher yardstick: the {{data.tumuli|int}} barrows and tumuli in OpenStreetMap that carry no megalithic tag, reweighted so that each country counts as much as it does among the megaliths. Against those, the edge shrinks: {{coast.groups.all.observed_near|pct0}} of megaliths against {{coast.groups.all.tumuli_near|pct0}} of barrows, a ratio of {{coast.groups.all.ratio_tumuli|f1}}. Because monuments cluster, I resampled 50 km grid cells rather than single sites to get an honest range for the megalith figure, and it runs from {{coast.groups.all.observed_near_ci.0|pct0}} to {{coast.groups.all.observed_near_ci.1|pct0}}, wide enough to include the barrows.

    04 · Test twoWhere are the oldest dates?

    Distance to the sea cannot tell us which way an idea moved. Dates can. I took the p3k14c database of archaeological radiocarbon dates, {{c14.dates_box|int}} dates in the map's frame, dropped {{c14.shell_dropped|int}} shell dates, and linked sites to megaliths by two documented rules: the site's name contains a megalithic word (dolmen, anta, mamoa, hunebed, long barrow, nuraghe and so on), or it shares a distinctive name with a mapped megalith within 1 km. That gave {{c14.primary_sites|int}} monuments. A check of {{c14.link_check.n}} links picked at random found {{show.link_ok}} that point at the same monument or the same named group, {{show.link_unclear|words}} that are unclear, usually a village name shared with a tomb nearby, and {{show.link_wrong|words}} that is wrong.

    I calibrated every date with the IntCal20 curve and took each monument's earliest date between 5500 and 500 BC. One early date proves little: it can come from charcoal that was old when the stones went up, or from activity before the monument. So for each region I used a steadier marker, the date by which the earliest tenth of its dated tombs had begun, with a bootstrap interval, and only for regions with at least eight dated tombs.

    Now the caution. The intervals are wide and overlap: north-west France could plausibly begin anywhere between about {{show.nwf_hi|yr}} and {{show.nwf_lo|yr}} BC on this evidence, and {{show.second_region}} between about {{show.second_hi|yr}} and {{show.second_lo|yr}} BC. And within regions, tombs nearer the coast are not earlier than tombs inland: the rank correlation between a tomb's start and its distance to the sea is {{c14.primary.spearman_start_vs_coast.rho|f2}} across {{c14.primary.spearman_start_vs_coast.n}} linked tombs, which is nothing. If boats carried the idea, they carried it between regions, not visibly from the beach inland.

    05 · Test threeThe Atlantic facade, type by type

    The third test asks where each kind of monument lives. I split Europe into three zones: within 50 km of the Atlantic and northern seas (including the Channel, the North Sea and the Baltic), within 50 km of the Mediterranean and Black Sea, and inland. Random land in the same countries puts {{show.z_rand_atl|pct0}} of points in the Atlantic zone; {{show.z_all_atl|pct0}} of megalithic monuments are there.

    Some types are emphatically Atlantic: {{show.z_longb_atl|pct0}} of long barrows, {{show.z_cpw_atl|pct0}} of Irish court, portal and wedge tombs, {{show.z_rows_atl|pct0}} of stone rows and alignments and {{show.z_circle_atl|pct0}} of stone circles. But dolmens, the commonest tomb, lie about half inland ({{show.z_dolmen_inland|pct0}}), and tombs as a whole are in the Atlantic zone no more often than ordinary barrows in the same countries ({{show.z_tomb_atl|pct0}} against {{show.z_tum_atl|pct0}}). The tombs' Atlantic flavour, in other words, is largely the Atlantic flavour of where Neolithic and Bronze Age people buried their dead at all.

    06 · VerdictSo, was there a highway?

    Each test leans the same way. Megaliths are more coastal than the land around them; the earliest dated tombs cluster in north-west France and the Atlantic regions start earlier than the rest; and the most distinctive types, stone rows, circles, long barrows and the Irish court tombs, belong to the Atlantic fringe. That is what a sea-borne idea would leave behind.

    But it is also what you would see if Atlantic Europe simply had more good stone, more people near the coast and more archaeologists with radiocarbon budgets. Megalithic tombs are only modestly more coastal than other burial mounds, and the regional dates rest on a few dozen tombs per region at best. The data fit the highway. They do not prove it was used.

    07 · CaveatsWhat the data can and cannot show

    Method in brief

    Monuments. OpenStreetMap objects tagged as megaliths (archaeological_site=megalith, megalith_type, or historic=menhir, standing_stone, stone_circle or dolmen), plus stone circles found by a multilingual name search and checked against their tags and names. Points within 15 m of another of the same type were merged, which removed {{data.duplicates_removed|int}} duplicates, mostly a node and an outline for one monument or the separate stones of one row. Outlines are placed at their first vertex. Only countries Natural Earth assigns to Europe are kept. Period labels come from OpenStreetMap tags and, where an object links to Wikidata, from Wikidata; {{show.not_recorded|int}} monuments have none.

    Coast. Distance to the Natural Earth 1:10m coastline, in an equal-area projection. Random points: ten per monument, uniform on land within the same country. Barrows: OpenStreetMap tumuli without a megalithic tag. Intervals resample 50 km grid cells.

    Dates. p3k14c (uncalibrated), marine shell removed, calibrated one by one with IntCal20 on a 5 year grid; I use the median. Links: a megalithic keyword in the site name, or a shared distinctive name with an OSM megalith within 1 km. Adding sites that only lie within 150 m of a megalith (a looser rule) keeps north-west France first. Regions are drawn with simple boxes and country borders, set out in the code.

    Coverage is uneven. OpenStreetMap reflects where volunteers have mapped and how they tag. France has {{show.france_n|int}} megaliths here, Denmark only {{show.denmark_n|int}} and Sweden {{show.sweden_n|int}}; Denmark instead has {{show.denmark_tumuli|int}} mounds tagged as plain tumuli, some of which will be megalithic tombs, and those sit in the barrow baseline. That is why every comparison is made within countries. Types are tagged inconsistently: one mapper's dolmen is another's chamber tomb, and {{data.by_class.unk|int}} monuments have no type at all.

    Radiocarbon coverage is uneven too. Britain and Ireland are dated far more densely than Iberia or Scandinavia, and Sardinia, Italy and south-east Europe have almost no dated tombs in the linked set. A region with few dates will look later than it was simply because its earliest tombs have not been dated. Earliest dates can come from material older than the monument; a 10th percentile softens this but does not remove it.

    Coasts move. Sea level and shorelines have changed since the Neolithic, especially around the North Sea and the Baltic, so present-day distances are only a guide.

    What this cannot show. Distance and dates are consistent with a maritime spread but cannot separate it from other explanations, such as people independently building in stone where good stone lay near the coast. This is a test of open data, not a substitute for the detailed regional chronologies archaeologists build site by site.
